# Chapter 8: Developing Judgment (Not Just Skills)

---

## The Manager's Dilemma

A director of analytics at a retail bank ran a team of twelve data professionals. For years, her development approach was straightforward and effective: give people progressively harder analytical challenges. Junior analysts started with routine reporting. As they demonstrated competence, they graduated to complex modelling. The best eventually owned strategic advisory for senior stakeholders. The path was clear. Execution mastery led to more demanding execution, which eventually led to advisory responsibility.

AI compressed the path. Junior analysts with AI assistance could now produce modelling work that previously required five years of technical development. The execution ramp, the mechanism through which she had always developed people, had been shortened from years to months. Her analysts could produce almost anything technically. What they could not do was determine whether what they produced was the right thing to produce.

A junior analyst delivered a comprehensive customer segmentation model. Technically flawless. Methodologically sound. Entirely useless. It answered a question that no one in the business was actually asking, using dimensions that did not map to any operational decision the bank could make. The model was correct. The judgment about what to model was absent.

Her development challenge had fundamentally shifted. She no longer needed to develop execution skill (AI handled the ramp). She needed to develop judgment: the ability to determine what analysis is worth doing, what question actually serves the business decision, what nuance in the data matters versus what is noise, and when an analysis is good enough to act on versus when it needs more depth.

Judgment had always been the destination of her development path. But the path itself, years of progressively harder execution, had been the mechanism that built judgment implicitly. The analyst who spent five years building models absorbed judgment through accumulated experience: seeing which models informed decisions and which gathered dust, observing which methodological choices mattered and which did not, developing the taste that distinguishes useful analysis from technically correct irrelevance.

With AI compressing the execution path, she needed to develop judgment explicitly rather than hoping it would emerge as a byproduct of time served.

## The Operating Shift

### The Judgment Development Gap

Traditional professional development is built almost entirely around skill acquisition: teaching people to do things. Write better code. Build better models. Design better interfaces. Present more effectively. Manage projects more efficiently.

Judgment, the ability to determine what is worth doing, evaluate whether it was done well, and decide what to do when the answer is ambiguous, has historically been treated as a byproduct of experience rather than a deliberately developable capability. "Good judgment comes from experience, and experience comes from bad judgment" is a folk wisdom that accurately describes how judgment developed in the execution-scarcity era: slowly, through accumulated trial and error over years.

AI disrupts this model in two ways:

**Disruption 1: The execution ramp no longer builds judgment at the same rate.**

When AI compresses years of execution development into months, people gain technical capability without the accumulated judgment that previously came bundled with it. You get analysts who can produce anything but cannot determine what to produce. Engineers who can build anything but cannot evaluate whether it should be built. Designers who can create anything but cannot judge whether it serves the user.

**Disruption 2: Judgment is now the primary human value contribution.**

When execution is abundant, the scarce human contribution is direction and judgment. This means developing judgment is no longer a "nice to have" development activity that happens naturally over time. It is the primary management development objective. If you are not deliberately building your team's judgment, you are not developing them for the roles they need to fill.

### The Judgment Architecture

Judgment is not a single capability. It is an architecture composed of five distinct components, each of which can be developed through targeted practice.

**Component 1: Problem Framing Judgment**

The ability to look at a situation and determine what the actual problem is before solving it. This includes: distinguishing symptoms from root causes, identifying which framing of the problem will lead to actionable solutions, and recognising when the stated problem is not the real problem.

Development method: Expose people to ambiguous situations and ask them to frame the problem before solving it. Evaluate and provide feedback on the framing quality, not just the solution quality.

**Component 2: Relevance Judgment**

The ability to determine what matters and what does not in a given context. This includes: selecting which data points to elevate, which analysis paths to pursue, which factors to weight heavily, and which to dismiss. In a world of infinite AI-generated options, relevance judgment, the ability to curate ruthlessly, becomes the primary intellectual contribution.

Development method: Present people with more information than they need and evaluate their ability to identify what matters. Give them ten AI-generated options and evaluate which three they select and why.

**Component 3: Quality Judgment**

The ability to evaluate whether output meets the required standard for the specific context and consequence level. This is not about absolute quality (is this perfect?) but about contextual quality (is this good enough for this purpose, this audience, and this consequence level?). Over-investment in quality for low-consequence work is as much a judgment failure as under-investment for high-consequence work.

Development method: Give people output at varying quality levels and ask them to evaluate fitness-for-purpose. Calibrate their internal standards against organisational requirements. Help them develop the ability to say "this is good enough" as confidently as they say "this needs more work."

**Component 4: Timing Judgment**

The ability to determine when to act, when to wait, when to iterate further, and when to ship. This includes: sensing when additional analysis would change the decision versus when it is merely comfort-seeking, recognising when a window of opportunity is closing, and understanding the cost of delay versus the cost of premature action.

Development method: Create time-pressured decisions where the person must commit with incomplete information. Then debrief: "Was that the right moment to decide? What would you have gained by waiting? What did you risk by acting?"

**Component 5: Contextual Judgment**

The ability to apply organisational, relational, political, and cultural knowledge to decisions. This is the judgment that determines not just "what is correct" but "what is correct here, for this stakeholder, in this political landscape, given this history." It is the most context-dependent form of judgment and the hardest to develop without direct experience.

Development method: Expose people to stakeholder contexts they do not normally access. Include them in cross-functional meetings. Share the "invisible context" behind your own decisions. Explain why a technically correct recommendation was modified for political or relational reasons.

## The Implementation Code

### The Judgment Stretch Assignment

Traditional stretch assignments challenge someone's execution capacity: a harder technical problem, a larger project, a tighter deadline. Judgment stretch assignments challenge someone's direction and evaluation capacity.

**Design Principles for Judgment Stretch Assignments:**

*Principle 1: Ambiguity is the essential ingredient.*
If the assignment has a clear right answer, it tests execution, not judgment. The assignment must require the person to determine what "right" looks like before they can produce it. Example: "Determine what analysis the leadership team actually needs to make the Q3 pricing decision" rather than "Build a pricing analysis."

*Principle 2: Multiple viable paths.*
The assignment should offer multiple legitimate approaches, each with different tradeoffs. The person must exercise judgment about which path to take and be prepared to justify the choice. Example: "We could approach this from a customer segment lens, a competitive dynamics lens, or a financial modelling lens. Each has strengths. Which framing will serve the decision best, and why?"

*Principle 3: Consequence must be real but bounded.*
Judgment development requires real stakes. If nothing depends on the person's judgment, they are not practicing judgment; they are doing exercises. But the consequences must be bounded enough that failure is survivable and instructive rather than catastrophic. Example: internal strategic recommendations rather than client-facing deliverables for their first judgment stretch.

*Principle 4: The evaluation focuses on the judgment, not just the outcome.*
Good judgment can produce bad outcomes (low-probability events happen). Bad judgment can produce good outcomes (luck). Evaluate the quality of the reasoning process and the decision logic, not just whether the result was positive. Debrief both successes and failures at the judgment level: "What did you consider? What did you weigh? What would you weigh differently if you could do it again?"

### The Judgment Development Conversation

Development conversations in the judgment architect model are fundamentally different from skill development conversations. You are not teaching someone how to do something. You are building their capacity to evaluate, decide, and direct.

**The Judgment 1:1 Structure (Monthly, 45 minutes):**

*Phase 1: Judgment Reflection (15 minutes)*

Questions:
- "Walk me through a decision you made this month where you had to exercise genuine judgment, where the answer was not obvious and you had to weigh competing factors."
- "What did you consider? What did you decide to prioritise and what did you decide to de-prioritise?"
- "With hindsight, do you still think your weighting was correct? What would you adjust?"

Your role: Listen. Ask clarifying questions. Help the person articulate their reasoning process explicitly. Many people exercise judgment instinctively but cannot articulate it, which means they cannot improve it deliberately.

*Phase 2: Judgment Calibration (15 minutes)*

Share your own reasoning on a recent judgment call:
- "Here's a decision I made this month and here's how I thought about it. [Describe the ambiguity, the factors weighed, the choice made, and the reasoning.]"
- "What would you have done differently? Why?"

Your role: Model judgment explicitly. Show your reasoning, including the uncertainty, the tradeoffs, and the moments where your judgment could have gone either way. This demystifies judgment and makes it something learnable rather than something mystical that only experienced people possess.

*Phase 3: Judgment Growth (15 minutes)*

Based on the discussion:
- "Here's the judgment component I think you should focus on developing next month: [specific component from the architecture]."
- "Here's an opportunity coming up where you can practice it: [specific situation or assignment]."
- "Here's what I'll be evaluating: not whether the outcome is perfect, but whether your reasoning demonstrates growth in [specific area]."

### The Team Judgment Practice

Individual judgment development is necessary but insufficient. Teams also need collective judgment: the ability to make sound decisions together, to challenge each other's reasoning, and to arrive at better conclusions than any individual would reach alone.

**The Weekly Judgment Session (30 minutes):**

Select one real decision, problem, or evaluation from the team's current work. Present it to the team with the relevant context but without a pre-made answer.

Structure:
1. **Framing (5 min):** The presenter describes the situation, the ambiguity, and the judgment required.
2. **Individual reasoning (5 min):** Each team member silently writes their judgment: what they would decide and why.
3. **Sharing (10 min):** Each person shares their reasoning (not just their conclusion).
4. **Calibration (5 min):** The team discusses where reasoning diverged. What factors did different people weight differently? Where does someone see something others missed?
5. **Decision (5 min):** The accountable person makes the final call, explaining which reasoning elements they are incorporating.

This practice builds three capabilities simultaneously:
- Individual judgment (through forced articulation of reasoning)
- Collective calibration (understanding how your judgment compares to peers')
- Healthy challenge norms (making it normal to question and be questioned on reasoning)

---

### The Feedback-on-Judgment Protocol

Giving feedback on judgment is harder than giving feedback on execution. Execution feedback is often objective: the code works or it does not, the report is complete or it is not. Judgment feedback is inherently subjective and must be delivered with precision to be developmental rather than demoralising.

**The structure for judgment feedback:**

1. **Name the judgment call.** "In the [specific situation], you had to decide [specific judgment]."

2. **Acknowledge the difficulty.** "This was genuinely ambiguous. There was no obvious right answer."

3. **Describe what you observed.** "I noticed you weighted [factor A] heavily and seemed to de-weight [factor B]."

4. **Share your perspective.** "In my experience, [factor B] tends to be more consequential in situations like this because [specific reasoning]. Here's why..."

5. **Invite reconsideration.** "If you encountered a similar situation next month with this additional perspective, what might you weight differently?"

6. **Affirm the growth trajectory.** "The fact that you took ownership of this decision and committed is exactly what I need from you. The calibration will improve with each iteration."

This structure separates the judgment from the person. You are not saying "your judgment is bad." You are saying "here is additional perspective that may sharpen your judgment in future situations." The former triggers defensiveness. The latter enables learning.

## The Organisational Context

Judgment development requires organisational conditions that many companies do not yet provide.

**The time investment challenge.** Judgment development takes time that produces no immediate output. Judgment 1:1s, team judgment sessions, and stretch assignments with debrief all consume hours that could be spent on delivery. If your organisation measures teams purely on throughput, judgment development competes with delivery metrics. Frame the investment in the language your leadership understands: "I am investing four hours per week in building the team's ability to ensure our output is aimed at the right targets. Without this investment, we produce volume without strategic alignment, which costs us more in rework and stakeholder dissatisfaction."

**The evaluation infrastructure gap.** Most HR systems have no mechanism for evaluating judgment quality. They evaluate output, behaviour, and sometimes competency demonstration. Judgment is invisible to standard evaluation frameworks. Until formal systems catch up, build your own: document judgment quality observations in your 1:1 notes, use the Judgment Development Matrix in performance conversations, and advocate for updated competency frameworks that include direction and evaluation capabilities.

**The career path implication.** If promotion criteria still reward execution mastery ("promoted to Senior because she delivered the most complex technical project"), judgment development feels like a career detour rather than career advancement. Work with HR and your leadership to create career paths that reward judgment development explicitly: "promoted to Senior because she consistently identified the right problems to solve and made sound decisions in ambiguous situations."

**The generational dynamic.** Junior team members who entered the workforce with AI tools from day one may have strong AI orchestration skills but limited judgment depth (because they skipped the execution years that previously built judgment implicitly). Senior team members may have deep judgment but resist AI adoption. Your development strategy must address both: accelerated judgment development for juniors who missed the execution ramp, and AI orchestration development for seniors whose judgment is valuable but whose workflow is legacy.

## The Operating Checklist

1. **Develop judgment explicitly.** In an AI-augmented environment, judgment no longer develops implicitly through years of execution experience. It must be a deliberate management development objective with specific activities, targeted practice, and structured feedback.

2. **Decompose judgment into components.** Problem Framing, Relevance, Quality, Timing, and Contextual judgment are distinct capabilities that develop independently. Assess each team member's profile and target the specific component that will unlock the most value.

3. **Design judgment stretch assignments.** Ambiguity, multiple paths, real but bounded consequence, and evaluation of reasoning quality rather than just outcome. These are the development experiences that build judgment under conditions that mimic real professional demands.

4. **Make judgment reasoning visible.** Through judgment 1:1s, team judgment sessions, and modelling your own reasoning explicitly. Judgment develops through articulation, calibration, and challenge, not through osmosis.

5. **Give feedback on the reasoning, not just the result.** Judgment feedback separates the decision logic from the person, shares additional perspective, invites reconsideration, and affirms the growth trajectory. The goal is sharpening the instrument, not criticising the musician.
